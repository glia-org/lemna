The morning started with rain against the kitchen window, and I made coffee before anyone else was awake. The house was quiet in the way it only is before seven, when the radiators tick and the street outside has not yet decided what kind of day it will be. I sat at the table with a notebook and tried to write down what I remembered of the dream, but most of it had already gone.

My grandmother used to say that the first hour of the day belongs to nobody. She meant that nothing you do in it can be taken from you, because nobody else has asked for it yet. I did not understand her when I was young. I thought she simply liked to get up early, as old people often do, and that the saying was a way to make a habit sound wise. Now I think she was right, and that she knew something about attention that took me thirty years to learn.

The town where she lived had one bakery, two churches and a river that flooded every few springs. Her house stood on the higher bank, so the water never reached it, but she kept sandbags in the shed anyway. When I asked her why, she said that a river does not read maps. I liked that answer so much that I wrote it on the inside cover of my school diary, where it stayed until the diary fell apart.

Teh bakery opened at six. The baker was a tall, quiet man who whistled while he worked and never seemed to look at the bread, as if his hands knew the dough better than his eyes did. On Saturdays my grandmother sent me down the hill with a cloth bag and a few coins, and I came back with a loaf that was still too hot to hold. We ate the end of it on the way home, which she pretended not to notice.

Years later, when I moved to the city, I tried to find bread like that and failed. The shops sold loaves that looked right and tasted of nothing. I complained about this to a friend, who told me that the bread had not changed, I had. She may have been right. Memory improves the things it keeps, the way a river smooths the stones it carries, until they no longer resemble the rocks they broke from.

That kind of noticing is what I have tried to bring to my own work. I write for a living, mostly reports and articles, and the hardest part is not finding the words. The hardest part is staying with a sentence long enough to hear whether it is true. A sentence can be grammatical, clear and pleasant to read, and still say something I do not believe. Catching that requires a kind of patience I do not always have.

One winter I had a idea for a longer piece, a book about small towns and the trades that once kept them alive. I spent months visiting places like my grandmother's town, talking to the last cobblers and clockmakers and printers, and filling notebooks with what they told me. Most of them were happy to talk. A few were suspicious, and one old printer refused to say anything at all until I had helped him clean the rollers of his press.

That afternoon with the printer taught me more than all the interviews together. He worked slowly and did not explain what he was doing, so I had to watch. After an hour I began to see the order in his movements, the way each step prepared the next. When we finished, he finally spoke, and what he said was simple: the work is the explanation. I have thought about that sentence ever since.

I have started writing again in the mornings, before work, in the first hour that belongs to nobody. I do not try to finish anything. I write whatever comes, without stopping to correct it, and I only read it back the next day. The rule is that I may not delete a word while I am writing. If a sentence goes wrong, I write a better one after it and keep going.

This habit has changed how I think about mistakes. When I cannot go back, I stop worrying about the last sentence and start paying attention to the next one. The draft is full of errors, of course, missing commas and doubled words and the occasional sentence that wanders off and never returns. But it is also full of things I would never have written if I had been allowed to stop and polish.

Afterwards I read the the draft with a pencil and fix what needs fixing. Most of the errors are small and easy to see. Some take longer, because the sentence is grammatical but wrong in a way that only becomes clear when I read it aloud. Those are the ones I have learned to value most, because they show me where I was thinking faster than I could write.

My grandmother kept a diary for most of her life. After she died, my mother found the volumes in a drawer, tied with string and labelled by year. We expected long entries and found short ones: the weather, what she cooked, who visited, whether the river was high. At first this disappointed me. Later I understood that she had written down exactly what she wanted to recieve back from the past, and nothing more.

I am trying to learn that kind of economy. A morning page does not need to be beautiful, clever or even complete, and nobody else ever has to read it. It needs to be honest, and it needs to exist. Everything else can come later, at the table with the pencil, when the rain has stopped and the street has finally decided what kind of day it will be.
