# Psychology of flow in writing, and why hiding already-written text helps a writer keep writing

Labels used in every bullet: **[E]** = empirical study (lab, classroom or field data); **[E-informal]** = data reported without controlled design or full statistics; **[T]** = theory or model claim; **[P]** = practitioner lore, product description, review or anecdote. The "Inferences" subsections are the researcher's own reasoning and carry no citation of their own; they point back to the findings they rest on. Where a number comes only from an abstract or a search-engine summary of an abstract (full text not read), that is said.

## 1. Freewriting and "invisible writing" (Elbow, Blau, replications): what was found about fluency, quality and anxiety?

### Takeaway
The founding evidence for "don't look back" is thin but consistent: Blau's invisible-writing work (empty pen on carbon paper; a dimmed screen with Marcus) is informal classroom data, mostly self-report, finding that writers stay coherent and concentrate better when they cannot reread. The one controlled lab study (Olive & Piolat 2002, handwriting) found text quality "little, if at all" affected while low-level execution got costlier. Freewriting practice reliably raises fluency (words per minute) in classroom studies, with some anxiety and attitude benefits, but there is no controlled evidence that it improves final-text quality.

### Cited Findings
- [P] Elbow's freewriting rule: write for ten minutes (later fifteen or twenty); "Never stop to look back, to cross something out, to wonder how to spell something"; when stuck, write a squiggle or repeat the last word; "The only requirement is that you never stop." — [Elbow 1973 excerpt, UCSB](https://www.research.ucsb.edu/sites/default/files/RD/docs/FREEWRITING-by-Peter-Elbow.pdf)
- [T/P] Elbow's mechanism: "Editing, in itself, is not the problem ... The problem is that editing goes on at the same time as producing"; freewriting "is nonediting" and, practised regularly, "undoes the ingrained habit of editing at the same time you are trying to produce"; "The habit of compulsive, premature editing doesn't just make writing hard. It also makes writing dead." He also says freewritings "help you by providing no feedback at all" — they must not "send any ripples back to you". — [Elbow 1973 excerpt](https://www.research.ucsb.edu/sites/default/files/RD/docs/FREEWRITING-by-Peter-Elbow.pdf)
- [E-informal] Blau (1983, ERIC ED232156): graduate students in a writing-pedagogy course did "invisible writing" on a sequence of increasingly demanding tasks (an "intellectual ascent" scale). Blau *expected* coherence to suffer more and more as tasks got harder because writers could not look back, but "the students continued to insist that they found invisible writing no impediment to their thinking or to the coherence of the texts". His reading: writers who cannot look back keep their sentences in mind "in a form resembling a reader's memory". He proposes that writers "blocked by their verbal self-consciousness or by a tendency to compulsive overediting" would produce coherent first drafts "more rapidly and copiously", and suggests invisible writing as a diagnostic for "wandering attention or the distractions of premature editing". — [ERIC ED232156](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=ED232156)
- [E-informal] Blau (1983, *College Composition and Communication* 34(3):297–312): classroom experiments with a carbon copy that prevents scanning while composing, used to show how tasks differ in cognitive demand and "how the need for scanning may indicate the cognitive difficulty of the task". — [ERIC EJ288026](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ288026); DOI [10.2307/358261](https://doi.org/10.2307/358261)
- [E-informal] Blau (1986, ERIC ED288201): method is an empty ballpoint pen on blank paper backed by carbon paper and a bottom sheet. An informal replication with graduate students was run because earlier studies had suggested invisible writing is *uncomfortable* (no pausing to reread); instead students "wrote competently and enthusiastically" and said the blank page made them concentrate more. "More formal experiments with over 100 inservice and preservice teachers" gave similar results, and "both objective measures and self-reports indicated that invisible writing helps writers focus on the task at hand". Blau calls it potentially "the best form of therapy for eliminating rereading and forcing unskilled writers to forge ahead in coherent continuous discourse". — [ERIC ED288201](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=ED288201)
- [E-informal/argument] Marcus & Blau (1983, *Educational Technology*), "Not Seeing Is Relieving: Invisible Writing with Computers": when students stop to edit language, syntax or mechanics, "the pauses obstruct fluency and dilute concentration"; they describe composing on word processors with the screen turned down, which shortens composing time and changes its "quantity and quality". — [ERIC EJ288461](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ288461)
- [E] Olive & Piolat (2002, *International Journal of Psychology* 37(4):209–218): writers composed a text and copied it with or without visual feedback (handwriting); processing load was measured with secondary auditory-probe reaction times. Removing visual feedback raised reaction-time interference *during handwriting in copying* but not *during pauses in composing*, i.e. it loaded execution, not high-level conceptual/linguistic processes; text quality was "little, if at all, affected by the suppression of visual feedback". Without visual feedback, writers also coordinated processes differently (more step-by-step rather than in parallel). — [Olive & Piolat 2002](https://doi.org/10.1080/00207590244000089)
- [P] University writing centres still teach the technique: "Turn your monitor off and type", set a fixed time, and resist "the temptation to turn the monitor on"; the aim is focus on ideas over spelling and punctuation. No research cited. — [Kent State Writing Commons](https://www.kent.edu/writingcommons/invisible-writing)
- [E] Freewriting practice and fluency: 39 first-year university (L2 English) students doing 70 ten-minute freewrites over a semester went from 19.5 to 30.1 words per minute, with significant gains every two weeks and higher output on timed TOEFL-style essays. — [Yasuda 2025, ERIC EJ1469720](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1469720)
- [E] 30 EFL university students: fluency rose across 540 freewrites; anxious students did worse on the writing test; students were generally positive about freewriting. — [Park 2020, ELT Journal, ERIC EJ1271373](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1271373). Similar in 17 preservice teachers (words per minute rose over 10 sessions; moderate L2 writing anxiety, mostly from low confidence). — [Park 2022, ERIC EJ1366107](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1366107)
- [E] Children (grades 1–4) who recorded their own word counts during freewriting wrote more words per session over time, along with more expressive writing and "little evidence of problematic side-effects" (four case studies). — [Moxley et al. 1995, ERIC EJ509963](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ509963)
- [E] Qualitative classroom study: focused freewriting ("writing without stopping and editing about a specific topic") used throughout an intensive academic-writing course produced themes of benefit in student feedback. — [Li 2007](https://doi.org/10.53761/1.4.1.5)
- [E] Caution, a different use of freewriting: as a *study strategy* after lectures, focused freewriting raised immediate comprehension, but one week later the group scored *below* a "focused thought" group. — [Hinkle & Hinkle 1990, ERIC EJ409484](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ409484)

### Inferences
- The best-supported claim is narrow: hiding prior text does **not** wreck coherence or quality for ordinary tasks (Blau's reports; Olive & Piolat's quality result). The "it frees writing" claim rests on self-report of concentration and on Blau's "objective measures", which are unpublished in the abstracts. Treat "more fluent" as plausible and "better text" as unproven.
- Olive & Piolat's cost fell on *execution* (handwriting without seeing the pen trace). Touch typists do not need to see the output to execute (see §3), so in a typing tool that cost should be smaller than in handwriting.
- Blau's framing points at a specific target group: writers who overedit or are blocked by "verbal self-consciousness". A forward-only tool is a remedy for them, not a general way to write better.

### Gaps
- The full text of Blau 1983 (CCC) and the ERIC papers was not retrieved (ERIC has no full text for these records). Sample sizes, the "objective measures" and any effect sizes are unknown. One search-engine summary said Blau found narrative and letters easy but theoretical argument and poetry hard without scanning; this could not be checked against the article and should not be relied on.
- No modern replication of invisible *composition* with keystroke logging (Inputlog, ScriptLog) or in CALL/L2 research was found. Typing studies that remove visual feedback exist (§3) but use copy-typing or single-word dictation, not composing texts.
- No controlled study found on whether freewriting improves final-text quality, as opposed to fluency.

## 2. Cognitive models (Hayes & Flower, Kellogg): how does reviewing or reading back compete with translating, and what supports "draft without editing"?

### Takeaway
Planning, translating (turning ideas into sentences) and reviewing all draw on one limited central executive, so rereading and fixing during drafting take capacity from producing text. Writers already put most corrections off until a sentence is done, and do so more under load. Yet rereading is not pure waste: eye-tracking shows look-backs cluster at sentence boundaries and seem to serve *planning*. The direct test of "rough draft without polishing" (Kellogg 1988) found it neither helped nor hurt quality; a short (even mental) outline beforehand did help.

### Cited Findings
- [E] Kellogg (2001): with college students writing by hand or on a word processor, planning, translating and reviewing appeared to draw on one shared, general-purpose working-memory resource (secondary-task reaction times). — [Kellogg 2001, Scholars' Mine](https://scholarsmine.mst.edu/psysci_facwork/304). A summary adds that when handwriting demands were low, reviewing gained more of the freed capacity than planning did. — [summary, stafforini.com](https://stafforini.com/works/kellogg-2001-competition-working-memory/)
- [T/E] Kellogg's working-memory model (1996), reviewed after 17 years: "strong support for the core assumption that planning, translating, and reviewing are all dependent on the central executive"; translation also needs verbal working memory; "the claim that editing makes no demands on the phonological loop is tenuous". — [Kellogg, Whiteford, Turner & Cahill 2013, J. Writing Research](https://doi.org/10.17239/jowr-2013.05.02.1)
- [T/E] Hayes & Flower's "monitor" (the executive that switches among writing processes), tested directly: writers could fix a planted error or finish the sentence first; per the abstract, 88% of trials finished the sentence first and 12% fixed the error first, and error-first happened significantly more under *low* memory load. The authors conclude the executive "schedules tasks to mitigate direct competition over working memory resources". — [Quinlan, Loncke, Leijten & Van Waes 2012, Written Communication 29:345–368 (abstract via Antwerp repository)](https://repository.uantwerpen.be/link/irua/100224)
- [E] Kellogg (1988): a written outline produced higher-rated documents than no outline; a **rough draft gave no quality benefit over a polished draft**; a *mental* outline helped as much as a written one; outlines worked by letting writers spend more processing time on translating. — [Kellogg 1988, JEP:LMC 14(2):355–365, abstract](https://scholarsmine.mst.edu/psysci_facwork/284)
- [E] An earlier letter-writing study by Kellogg (1987) reportedly found rough vs polished drafting changed *when* writers reviewed, not quality or efficiency (from a search summary; not read in full). — [Kellogg, "Writing Performance: Effects of Cognitive Strategies", Scholars' Mine](https://scholarsmine.mst.edu/psysci_facwork/286)
- [E] Eye-tracking of adults writing short expository texts: writers spent about 13% of their time looking back into their own text; look-backs were "strongly predicted by linguistically important boundaries", above all just before a new sentence; 36% of look-back sequences were sustained reading, the rest "hopping" between words; the authors argue these fixations mainly serve *planning* ongoing production, not error detection. — [Torrance, Johansson, Johansson & Wengelin 2016, Psychological Research 80(5):729–743](https://irep.ntu.ac.uk/id/eprint/16307/)
- [E] Writers who watched the monitor (vs the keyboard) typed significantly faster, were more productive, reread their text more and often read *while* writing; keyboard gazers used the cursor keys more (a more serial revision style); final texts did not differ between the groups. — [Johansson, Wengelin, Johansson & Holmqvist 2010, Reading and Writing 23:835–851](https://www.lunduniversity.lu.se/lup/publication/76c41904-2e75-40b5-bae4-39e2791a26eb)
- [E] Eye-tracking of 7th–8th graders: fluent and less fluent readers did not differ in reading-during-writing behaviour; both groups heavily monitored their most recent words (thesis; from search summary). — [Beers, University of Washington thesis](https://digital.lib.washington.edu/researchworks/items/7e911f79-0e60-4a97-800e-e698425c7c22)
- [T/E] Galbraith's dual-process account: discovery (writers finding out what they think) comes from spontaneous, implicitly guided sentence production, not only from rhetorical planning. With low and high self-monitors under two planning conditions and keystroke logging, "discovery was related to spontaneous sentence production and global revision of text, which had opposing relationships with text quality". — [Baaijen & Galbraith 2018, Cognition and Instruction](https://doi.org/10.1080/07370008.2018.1456431); origin: [Galbraith 1992, Instructional Science](https://doi.org/10.1007/bf00119655)
- [T] Keystroke-logging research treats writing fluency as several dimensions: production, process variation, revision and pause behaviour (plus source fragmentation). — [Van Waes et al. 2013, Tijdschrift voor Taalbeheersing](https://aup-online.com/content/journals/10.5117/TVT2013.2.WAES)
- [E] Spelling and grammar checkers, in a *revision* task (65 students fixing planted errors in unfamiliar essays), helped surface fixes and did not reduce content revisions. — [Figueredo & Varnhagen 2006, BJET](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-8535.2006.00562.x)

### Inferences
- Shared-capacity models predict what writers report: every look-back-and-fix during drafting takes executive capacity from producing text. A tool that makes old text unavailable removes the *opportunity* for reviewing rather than relying on the writer's willpower.
- Writers already prefer to put fixes off (88% sentence-first). A forward-only tool works *with* the executive's own scheduling, not against it; what it removes is the visible prompt (a typo on screen) that pulls reviewing forward.
- Look-backs at sentence starts appear to help plan the next sentence (Torrance). So hiding *everything* may cost some planning support, most of all for argumentative or complex text (also Blau's original hypothesis). A visible "frontier" (current clause or sentence) keeps local planning support and removes the global rereading that leads to editing.
- Johansson et al. are a warning against an over-literal reading: looking at the screen was associated with *more* productivity. The goal is not to stop the eyes resting on the text; it is to make sure what they rest on is the frontier, not editable history. (Confound: keyboard gazers are usually weaker touch typists.)
- Kellogg 1988 sets expectations: forward-only drafting should not be sold as improving quality. Its case rests on fluency, experience, idea discovery (Galbraith) and reaching a full draft. A 30-second "intent" line before the session (a mental outline) is the one pre-writing step with quality evidence.

### Gaps
- No study found that compares, in composing (not copying), a visible-history condition with a hidden-history condition and measures both words per minute and quality with modern keystroke logging.
- Torrance 2016's breakdown of *which* text look-backs target (last words vs earlier sentences) was not in the accessible abstract.
- Galbraith 1992's specific results for low vs high self-monitors were not retrieved (no abstract available).

## 3. Typing-lab evidence: what happens when typists cannot see what they type, or see only the last word?

### Takeaway
Skilled typing runs on two loops: an inner, keystroke-level loop that does not need the screen, and an outer loop that watches the screen. Taking away the screen echo barely changes accuracy and slows typing slightly, but cuts *corrections* sharply (42% to 9% of errors in one study). Typists still notice about half their errors; they just stop fixing them. Making typists attend explicitly to the mechanics of typing is strongly disruptive. Text that moves under the eyes is hard to read.

### Cited Findings
- [E] Typing to dictation with immediate vs delayed (end-of-trial) visual feedback, fast typists (mean 86.4 wpm), 5,632 responses: accuracy did not differ (72.5% vs 76.5%); inter-key intervals were slightly slower without feedback (125 vs 134 ms); **42% of errors were corrected with feedback vs 9% without**; yet in the no-feedback condition 54% of errors were reported in metacognitive judgments, i.e. noticed but not fixed. The authors note that in typing "visual feedback can be easily delayed without adverse effects". — [Pinet & Nozari 2020, preprint PDF](https://www.cmu.edu/dietrich/psychology/nozarilab/papers/2020PinetNozari.pdf)
- [E] On a flat keyboard (12 touch typists), showing asterisks instead of the typed letters did not change typing speed (F(1,108)=.05); seeing letters gave a higher corrected-error rate and a lower uncorrected-error rate, i.e. **visible text leads to more fixing, not faster typing**. — [Kim & Tan 2014, IEEE Haptics Symposium](https://microsoft.com/en-us/research/wp-content/uploads/2014/08/C63_JRKimTan_HS2014.pdf)
- [E] Removing the screen echo lengthened the time to the first keystroke, slowed inter-key intervals when explicit error monitoring was required, and made explicit error reports less accurate; implicit post-error slowing was unaffected by screen or hand visibility. Covering the hands also slowed typing. — [Snyder, Logan & Yamaguchi 2015, Attention, Perception & Psychophysics](https://doi.org/10.3758/s13414-014-0756-6); [abstract](https://research.edgehill.ac.uk/en/publications/watch-what-you-type-the-role-of-visual-feedback-from-the-screen-a-2/)
- [E] Two error detectors: when the experimenters silently corrected typists' errors or inserted false ones on screen, typists claimed authorship of the screen (took credit for corrected errors, blamed themselves for inserted ones), but their fingers slowed after *real* errors only, showing a keystroke-based detector that works without the screen. — [Logan & Crump 2010, Science](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1190483)
- [E] Attention to the mechanics disrupts: 16 touch typists (mean 68 wpm) cued to type only one hand's letters, even when the keystrokes were identical to whole-word typing, slowed first-keystroke latency by 233 ms (28%) and inter-key intervals by 34 ms (20%), and doubled the error rate (+.09); with inhibition required, inter-key intervals rose 104% and errors 200%. Cueing the same letters by colour was not disruptive. — [Logan & Crump 2009, Psychological Science](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9280.2009.02442.x); [PDF](https://www.crumplab.com/publications/Crump/files/3906/Logan%20and%20Crump%20-%202009%20-%20The%20left%20hand%20doesn't%20know%20what%20the%20right%20hand%20Is%20.pdf)
- [E] "Monitoring to report" details of one's own typing was disruptive even without any need to inhibit; holding information in working memory caused much less disruption; the authors' account includes explicit monitoring of normally implicit details. — [Snyder & Logan 2013, JEP:HPP](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0031007)
- [E] Degrading the usual haptic feedback (laser-projection keyboard) forced skilled typists to monitor their keystrokes. — [Yamaguchi & Logan 2013, JEP:HPP](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0033809)
- [E] Text that moves right-to-left in jumps ("leading" display) was read with poorer comprehension than a static page; jumps of 1–2 characters were worse than jumps of 4–10. — [Granaas et al. 1984, Human Factors](https://doi.org/10.1177/001872088402600109)
- [E] Ongoing motion does not by itself capture attention; the *onset* of motion does. — [Abrams & Christ 2003, Psychological Science](https://doi.org/10.1111/1467-9280.01458)

### Inferences
- These findings explain the mechanism behind invisible writing better than the composition studies do: removing visible text mainly removes the *trigger for correction* (outer loop), at a small speed cost, while the inner loop keeps typing fluent. That is the effect a forward-only tool wants.
- Since 54% of errors are still *noticed* without feedback, writers will feel typos they cannot see or fix. That may be the main source of residual unease in blind writing; a tool can acknowledge it (e.g. allow a fix inside the current word, or promise a clean-up pass) rather than pretend it is gone.
- Logan & Crump / Snyder & Logan suggest anything that invites explicit, per-keystroke monitoring (red squiggles, live character counts, autocorrect pop-ups, visible typo highlights) is a candidate disrupter of the inner loop. This is an extrapolation from copy-typing to composing.
- The user's VS Code observation (no wrap, caret pinned, text flowing left, attention on the last word) matches three findings: the eyes stay on the frontier (Johansson's productive monitor gazing), old text leaves the fovea fast and is hard to reread while it moves (Granaas), and steady self-caused motion is not an attention-grabbing onset (Abrams & Christ). That it "immensely helps" is n = 1 self-report.

### Gaps
- All typing studies above use copy-typing, single words or short phrases, not multi-sentence composition; no study was found that measures composition fluency with a horizontally scrolling single line.
- No study found on the *subjective* effect (momentum, enjoyment) of watching one's text slide away.

## 4. Flow theory (Csikszentmihalyi): its conditions, how a typing tool can supply each, and which feedback helps or hurts

### Takeaway
Flow is said to need clear proximal goals, immediate feedback and a challenge matched to skill, and to involve loss of self-consciousness. Only the challenge–skill link has been validated in controlled experiments. Evidence from reward research and progress-bar research points the same way: feedback that *informs* (this keystroke registered, you are moving) supports motivation, while feedback that *evaluates* or shows lagging progress undermines it. Neuroimaging of improvisation links spontaneous production to reduced activity in prefrontal self-monitoring regions. That fits the "inner critic" story but is correlational and comes from music and rap, not writing.

### Cited Findings
- [T] The three flow conditions in Nakamura & Csikszentmihalyi (2002): clear proximal goals, immediate feedback on progress, and challenges matched to skill; "only the link between optimal challenges and flow has been validated by controlled experiments (Keller & Bless, 2008)". — [Schaffer & Feng, AMCIS 2015](https://aisel.aisnet.org/amcis2015/HCI/GeneralPresentations/12)
- [T] Eight commonly listed flow components: challenge–skill balance, concentration on the present task, clear goals, immediate feedback, deep effortless involvement, sense of control, "decreased self-consciousness during flow, which becomes stronger afterward", altered time perception. — [Zhong 2025, systematic review](https://doi.org/10.2991/978-2-38476-382-5_81)
- [E] Larson's chapter "Flow and writing" studies how "psychic order and disorder" affect writing an original English composition: how far anxiety or boredom interfere with the task and how flow relates to finishing it successfully (abstract only; results not accessible). — [Larson 1988, in *Optimal Experience*, Cambridge UP](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/abs/optimal-experience/flow-and-writing/034F803D36BCEFB2AC36CF4679A93529)
- [E] Meta-analysis of 128 studies: engagement-, completion- and performance-contingent rewards undermined free-choice intrinsic motivation (d = −0.40, −0.36, −0.28); positive feedback *enhanced* free-choice behaviour (d = 0.33) and self-reported interest (d = 0.31). — [Deci, Koestner & Ryan 1999, Psychological Bulletin](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.125.6.627)
- [E] Progress indicators (online questionnaires): early feedback showing *slow* progress raised abandonment and worsened experience; intermittent feedback reduced the cost of discouraging feedback while keeping the benefit of encouraging feedback; "when progress seems to lag behind what users expect, feedback degrades their experience and lowers completion rates". — [Conrad et al. 2010, Interacting with Computers](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.intcom.2010.03.001)
- [E] Children recording their own freewriting word counts increased output without obvious side effects (see §1). — [Moxley et al. 1995, ERIC EJ509963](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ509963)
- [E] fMRI of professional jazz pianists: improvisation (vs over-learned sequences) showed widespread deactivation of dorsolateral prefrontal and lateral orbital cortex with focal medial prefrontal activation, interpreted as behaviour unfolding "in the absence of central processes that typically mediate self-monitoring and conscious volitional control". — [Limb & Braun 2008, PLoS ONE](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0001679)
- [E] Same dissociation in freestyle rap ("in the absence of conscious monitoring and volitional control"). — [Liu et al. 2012, Scientific Reports](https://doi.org/10.1038/srep00834)
- [E] fMRI of 28 participants writing a creative story continuation by hand vs copying showed hippocampal, temporal-pole and posterior-cingulate activity (free association, episodic retrieval); the paradigm did not isolate self-monitoring. — [Shah et al. 2011, Human Brain Mapping](https://doi.org/10.1002/hbm.21493)
- [T] Dietrich's neurocognitive account of flow (the "transient hypofrontality" hypothesis) proposes reduced explicit, prefrontal control during flow (abstract not retrieved; claim stated from the title and the well-known hypothesis). — [Dietrich 2004, Consciousness and Cognition](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.concog.2004.07.002)

### Inferences
How a typing tool can supply each condition:
- **Clear proximal goal**: one goal per session, set before starting (time box or word target, plus an optional one-line intent). The proximal goal while typing is "the next word"; the pinned caret keeps that the only goal in view.
- **Immediate feedback**: every keystroke should give an *informational* echo: the glyph appears at the caret, the line slides, optionally a click. That is feedback that the action registered (agency), not a judgement of quality. Deci's positive-feedback effect and the flow condition both point here.
- **Challenge–skill balance** (the only validated condition): for drafting, the challenge is mostly set by the topic and the target. Session length and target should be adjustable and start small (Conrad: progress that lags expectations demotivates). A pace the writer cannot meet turns into anxiety.
- **Loss of self-consciousness**: remove the mirror. No visible history, no squiggles, no live word count to evaluate oneself against, no reading-back. That is the part hiding text supplies directly.
- **Good feedback** (inference from above): keystroke echo at the caret, smooth motion, a soft click, a quiet time-progress cue (e.g. a slowly filling line), a word count shown only at the end or intermittently.
- **Bad feedback**: red squiggles or autocorrect (invite explicit monitoring, §3), a live word count or WPM ticking every keystroke (evaluative; slow early progress is demotivating per Conrad), streak-loss alarms, flashing warnings (motion onsets capture attention, §3).
- The prefrontal-deactivation studies fit the "inner critic quietens in flow" story, but they are correlational, from music and rap, and do not show that hiding text causes the state.

### Gaps
- Larson's results (how flow, anxiety and boredom relate to paper quality) could not be read; a secondary claim that students in flow wrote better than anxious or bored ones could not be verified (source blocked).
- No controlled study found showing that immediate feedback *causes* flow (the AMCIS paper testing it was withdrawn). Sample sizes for the Limb & Braun and Liu studies were not checked.
- No study found on flow measured during *typed* composition with keystroke logging.

## 5. Auditory and haptic keystroke feedback: does clicking help typing performance, perceived fluency or enjoyment?

### Takeaway
The controlled evidence is about keyboards that have **no physical click** (flat, zero-travel or touchscreen). There, added feedback helps: haptic most, sound less. Sound alone is not significantly better than nothing in some comparisons, but users prefer it to silence. Latency matters: audio feels simultaneous at ~20 ms and perceived quality drops between 70 and 100 ms. No controlled study was found on adding synthetic typewriter or mechanical clicks to an ordinary physical keyboard, or on clicks and writing fluency. That benefit rests on anecdote and on the user's own experience. Haptics from a Mac app are effectively unavailable while typing.

### Cited Findings
- [E] Flat keyboard, 12 touch typists (≥50 wpm), asterisk-display set: mean speed 65.6 wpm on a physical keyboard, 55.6 with local haptic clicks, 52.4 global haptic, 51.1 beep (auditory only), 43.1 with no feedback; total error 5.1, 7.0, 7.2, 8.2 and 11.7%. In post-hoc groups, beep fell in the same speed group as no feedback, and haptic intensity mattered while auditory intensity did not. — [Kim & Tan 2014, IEEE Haptics Symposium](https://microsoft.com/en-us/research/wp-content/uploads/2014/08/C63_JRKimTan_HS2014.pdf)
- [E] Flat keyboard: all haptic keyclick types increased speed and reduced errors vs none; "auditory keyclick feedback alone is not as effective as haptic keyclick feedback", adding audio to haptic added nothing significant, and "participants preferred auditory or haptic keyclick feedback to no feedback". — [Ma, Edge, Findlater & Tan 2015, IEEE WHC](https://doi.org/10.1109/whc.2015.7177717)
- [E] Touchscreen text entry: adding tactile feedback significantly improved finger typing, bringing it close to a physical keyboard. — [Hoggan, Brewster & Johnston 2008, CHI](https://doi.org/10.1145/1357054.1357300)
- [E] Latency: point of subjective simultaneity was 5 ms for tactile, 19 ms for audio, 32 ms for visual feedback; perceived button quality dropped significantly between 70 and 100 ms latency for tactile or audio, and between 100 and 150 ms for visual. — [Kaaresoja, Brewster & Lantz 2014, ACM TAP](https://doi.org/10.1145/2611387). Kim & Tan cite an earlier study in which half of 12 participants noticed a feedback delay between 98 and 118 ms. — [Kim & Tan 2014](https://microsoft.com/en-us/research/wp-content/uploads/2014/08/C63_JRKimTan_HS2014.pdf)
- [E] Enhanced auditory feedback on physical keyboards has been studied for typing force, EMG and comfort (Gerard et al. 2002), but the abstract and results were not retrieved. — [Gerard, Armstrong, Rempel & Woolley 2002, Applied Ergonomics 33(2):129–138](https://doi.org/10.1016/s0003-6870(01)00062-x)
- [E] Keystroke-sound pleasantness can be predicted from acoustic metrics (listening tests, product-sound-quality work). — [Boulandet et al. 2008, EPFL](https://infoscience.epfl.ch/record/118562/)
- [E] ASMR survey: common triggers include whispering, personal attention, "crisp sounds" and slow movements; the number of effective triggers was linked to higher flow scores (self-selected online sample). — [Barratt & Davis 2015, PeerJ](https://doi.org/10.7717/peerj.851)
- [E] Ambient noise: moderate (70 dB) vs low (50 dB) noise improved creative-task performance via more abstract processing; high noise (85 dB) hurt. — [Mehta, Zhu & Cheema 2012, J. Consumer Research](https://doi.org/10.1086/665048). In children, moderate (64 dB) classroom noise never helped creativity and hurt younger children with weak selective attention. — [Massonnié et al. 2019, Frontiers in Psychology](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2019.00381)
- [P] Klack (macOS menubar app) plays mechanical-switch sounds with no noticeable lag; some users say it "helps them work better"; a commenter finds many people's clacking in a shared office hard to concentrate through. Free alternatives: Mechvibes, KeyBell. — [9to5Mac 2023](https://9to5mac.com/2023/04/05/klack-app-mechanical-keyboard-sound-effects-for-mac/); [Creative Bloq](https://creativebloq.com/news/klack-macbook-mechanical-keyboard); [Boing Boing thread](https://bbs.boingboing.net/t/klack-a-mac-app-that-emits-a-satisfying-sound-whenever-you-press-a-key/256596)
- [P] Keyboard-enthusiast forum: a product claim that "a completely silent keyboard" slows typing is unsourced; a poster recalls a study in which a constant click delay was adapted to by experienced typists but not by novices, and notes feedback must follow actuation immediately (no citation given). — [Deskthority thread](https://deskthority.net/viewtopic.php?p=447115)
- [P] OmmWriter (full-screen writing app with backgrounds, ambient audio and keystroke sounds): small set of user reviews say keystroke sounds and music create a calm, productive mood; one says the music was too loud relative to the key sounds. — [Capterra reviews](https://www.capterra.co.uk/reviews/151933/ommwriter)
- [E/P] macOS haptics: Force Touch trackpads give haptic feedback only while the user touches the trackpad, and the system may override calls; developers report no way around that. — [Apple, NSHapticFeedbackPerformer.perform](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/appkit/nshapticfeedbackperformer/perform(_:performancetime:)); [Apple Developer Forums](https://developer.apple.com/forums/thread/70605)

### Inferences
- On a Mac's physical keyboard the fingers already get tactile and acoustic feedback, so the flat-keyboard performance gains do not transfer. Any benefit of a synthetic click is likely *affective* (pleasure, rhythm, "presence" of each word), consistent with users preferring feedback to none (Ma et al.) and with the user's own report. Design it as an enjoyment and agency cue, not a speed aid.
- Latency budget for the click and the glyph: aim under ~20–30 ms from key-down; above ~70 ms the sound would feel detached (Kaaresoja).
- Because sound intensity did not affect performance (Kim & Tan) and loud sound can annoy, keep it quiet, slightly varied per key (fewer identical repetitions; an assumption, not tested), and easy to turn off. Offer a typewriter-bell-style cue only at the session end, not at line ends (no lines in a single-line design).
- Ambient soundscapes: weak support for moderate background noise in creative tasks (one adult study, a null result in children). An optional low-level ambience is defensible; default off is safer.
- Haptics are not a usable channel for keystroke feedback on macOS (trackpad only, finger-on only).
- "Dopamine" or reward framing for clicks: no evidence found; avoid claiming it.

### Gaps
- No controlled study found on synthetic key sounds added to a physical keyboard and their effect on speed, errors, fluency or enjoyment; none on typewriter sounds specifically.
- No evidence found on rhythm entrainment between key sounds and typing tempo.
- Gerard et al. 2002 results not retrieved.

## 6. Punishment-based vs reward-based tools, timeboxing, warm-ups and "morning pages"

### Takeaway
The only controlled data on punishment-driven writing (Boice 1983) shows punishment raises *output* the most but halves *ideas per page*, and Boice later recommended the "least pressure sufficient". Regular scheduled sessions with modest self-recording did best on efficiency. Punishment apps (Most Dangerous Writing App, Flowstate, Write or Die's kamikaze mode) are described by users as stressful but effective in the moment; no study of them was found. Reward research warns against expected, contingent rewards and favours informational positive feedback. Timeboxing has mixed evidence. Morning pages have no trials.

### Cited Findings
- [E] Boice (1983), re-analysed by Krashen (2002): faculty volunteers with doctorates who "complained of difficulties in completing written projects", randomly assigned. Pages per day: no writing .1–.2; spontaneous ("when I feel like it") .3–.4; regular daily writing without incentives .9; **forced** writing (3 pages per session or donate $15 to a "despised organization") 3.2. Creative ideas per day: .06–.09, .21–.32, .63, 1.39. **Ideas per page: spontaneous .8, regular .7, forced .43**; Krashen: the punished group "barely met the goal, apparently writing just enough to avoid punishment", at half the efficiency. Boice (1994, quoted there) recommends the "least pressure sufficient" and extreme punishments only "as a last resort, temporarily; with longer use, their aversiveness can generalize to the act of writing itself". Writers who regularly recorded output and met a therapist kept steady output for a year (Boice 1989). — [Krashen 2002, Education 122(3):605–608](https://mail.sdkrashen.com/content/articles/optimal_levels.pdf)
- [P] The Most Dangerous Writing App (Manuel Ebert, open source, 2016): deletes all progress if typing stops for five seconds; sessions by time or word count; saving is allowed only after completing; "hardcore mode" blurs everything already written, so nothing can be edited until the session ends; aim stated as shutting down the inner editor to reach flow. — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Most_Dangerous_Writing_App). Wired, quoted: the screen edges turn red when you pause. — [Writers Write](https://www.writerswrite.co.za/have-you-tried-the-most-dangerous-writing-app/)
- [P] User accounts of the Most Dangerous Writing App: panic about losing a paragraph mid-session, yet a kind of relief from the pressure — [LessWrong review](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/5cJq8wGw2WACm9gHR/review-the-most-dangerous-writing-app); "kinda stressful but it really works" — [Adactio](https://adactio.com/links/10339)
- [P] Flowstate (Mac/iOS, 2016): text disappears if you stop for more than five (or seven, by source) seconds; sessions of 5 minutes to 3 hours; creators say it tests performing under pressure. — [Mental Floss](https://www.mentalfloss.com/technology/app-deletes-your-work-if-you-stop-writing); [No Film School](https://nofilmschool.com/2016/02/you-have-exactly-five-seconds-writing-app-flowstate-deletes-everything-you-just-wrote)
- [P] Write or Die: gentle mode (reminder box), normal mode (unpleasant sound until you resume), kamikaze mode (text erases itself word by word). — [Poetry Foundation 2012](https://www.poetryfoundation.org/harriet/2012/06/some-practical-ways-to-keep-writing-or-die); [Fiction Writers Review](https://fictionwritersreview.com/shoptalk/writeor-die/). Version 2 adds a "reward mode" ("positive reinforcement") with reward images at a frequency set on a slider. — [Write or Die v2](https://v2.writeordie.com)
- [P] Ilys: shows only the most recent keystroke plus a word-count progress counter; editing and deleting are blocked until the goal is met, then the full text becomes editable. — [The Next Web 2015](https://thenextweb.com/news/write-drunk-edit-sober); [Lecturalia 2015](https://www.lecturalia.com/blog/2015/11/26/ilys-lleva-al-limite-el-escribir-sin-distracciones/)
- [E] Rewards: expected, contingent rewards undermine intrinsic motivation (d ≈ −0.28 to −0.40); informational positive feedback enhances it (d ≈ +0.33). — [Deci, Koestner & Ryan 1999](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.125.6.627)
- [E] Timeboxing: 87 university students in real self-study sessions; fixed breaks (24 min work / 6 min break "Pomodoro", or 12/3) vs self-regulated breaks: self-regulators took longer sessions and breaks and reported more fatigue and distraction and less concentration and motivation; task completion was the same, so fixed breaks reached it in less time. — [Biwer et al. 2023, BJEP](https://doi.org/10.1111/bjep.12593). Contrary result: 176 students in a reading task, self-regulated breaks gave *more* positive moods (energy, concentration, motivation) with no performance difference. — [Göksu, Wiradhany & de Bruin 2026, Behavioral Sciences](https://doi.org/10.3390/bs16071158)
- [P] Session length: Elbow's freewriting starts at 10 minutes, later 15–20, at least three times a week. — [Elbow 1973 excerpt](https://www.research.ucsb.edu/sites/default/files/RD/docs/FREEWRITING-by-Peter-Elbow.pdf). [E] 10-minute sessions repeated 70 times raised L2 fluency by about half. — [Yasuda 2025](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1469720)
- [P] Morning pages (three longhand pages each morning, Julia Cameron): a critical review notes no randomised trial has tested them, and the Pennebaker expressive-writing studies often cited use a different protocol (about 15 minutes on several days about difficult experiences) that does not transfer. Blog source, weak. — [Thalira review of *The Artist's Way*](https://thalira.com/blogs/quantum-codex/artists-way-julia-cameron)

### Inferences
- Punishment buys quantity at the cost of idea density and, per Boice, risks making writing itself aversive. For a calm forward-only tool, deletion-on-pause runs against the "calm" goal. If offered at all, it should be opt-in and gentle (a fading nudge), never destroying text.
- The best-supported "pressure" is the mildest: a scheduled session and a private record of output (Boice/Krashen). That favours a session log (date, minutes, words) over streak-loss mechanics.
- Write or Die-style reward images are expected, contingent rewards; the meta-analysis suggests they may undermine intrinsic motivation. A plain informational end-of-session summary ("23 min, 812 words") fits the positive-feedback finding better.
- Ilys's design (last keystroke only; editing unlocked at goal) is the closest existing analogue to the proposed tool; its reveal-at-goal is a ready-made ending ritual.
- Timeboxing: a fixed session length is reasonable, but evidence on fixed vs self-chosen breaks is split; let the writer pick the length, with a sensible default (10–25 min).

### Gaps
- No study found of any punishment or reward writing app (MDWA, Flowstate, Write or Die, Ilys): no data on output, quality, anxiety or retention.
- No evidence found on warm-up prompts before freewriting, apart from Kellogg's outline result (§2).
- Boice's idea quality (as opposed to count) under punishment was not measured.

## 7. Anxiety, perfectionism and the inner critic: what reduces them (hiding text, no backspace, no spell check, delayed review)?

### Takeaway
Blocked writers are marked by rigid rules ("get the first paragraph right"), premature editing and verbal self-consciousness; regular low-pressure writing and techniques that stop rereading are the recurring remedies. The direct evidence that hiding text reduces *anxiety* is limited to Blau's self-reports; no controlled study was found for no-backspace, no-spell-check or delayed review as anxiety reducers. The mechanism evidence (explicit monitoring disrupts skilled production) is strong but comes from typing labs.

### Cited Findings
- [E] Rose (1980), interviews with ten competent UCLA writers, five fluent and five moderately to severely blocked: blocking is tied to "rigid rules" and "inflexible plans"; example of a blocked student writing and erasing her first paragraph for hours, telling herself "No. You can't say that. You'll bore them to death." — [Rose 1980, CCC](https://doi.org/10.2307/356589)
- [E-informal] Blau: invisible writing is proposed for writers blocked "by their verbal self-consciousness or by a tendency to compulsive overediting", and as therapy for "eliminating rereading". — [ERIC ED232156](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=ED232156); [ERIC ED288201](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=ED288201)
- [P/T] Elbow: schooling makes writers "obsessed with the 'mistakes'"; "trying to get the beginning just right is a formula for failure". — [Elbow 1973 excerpt](https://www.research.ucsb.edu/sites/default/files/RD/docs/FREEWRITING-by-Peter-Elbow.pdf)
- [E] Regular scheduled writing beats waiting for inspiration in both output and ideas, without the efficiency loss of punishment; "inspiration does not typically precede writing in experienced writers; it is the result of writing". — [Krashen 2002 on Boice](https://mail.sdkrashen.com/content/articles/optimal_levels.pdf)
- [E] Survey of 146 writers: those who wrote daily reported shorter blocks. — [Ahmed 2019, UNF thesis](https://digitalcommons.unf.edu/etd/903) (abstract only)
- [E] Freewriting in EFL classes: anxious students performed worse; freewriting practice raised fluency and was viewed positively. — [Park 2020](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1271373); [Park 2022](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1366107)
- [E] Explicit attention to the details of one's performance disrupts skilled typing (+20% inter-key interval, double errors). — [Logan & Crump 2009](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9280.2009.02442.x); [Snyder & Logan 2013](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0031007)
- [E] Without visual feedback, correction falls from 42% to 9% of errors. — [Pinet & Nozari 2020](https://www.cmu.edu/dietrich/psychology/nozarilab/papers/2020PinetNozari.pdf)
- [E] Spell and grammar checkers did not inhibit content revision in a *revision* task (not drafting). — [Figueredo & Varnhagen 2006](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-8535.2006.00562.x)
- [P] No-backspace modes exist: Draft's "Hemingway Mode" turns off deleting so you can only add at the end, framed as keeping writing apart from editing ("difficult to write if you're in a editing mindset") — [Kontny, Draft announcement](https://ninjasandrobots.com/draft-announcements-hemingway-mode-wordgoogle-docs-support-kindle-exports-and-more); "you can't backspace at all" — [Freelancers Union blog](https://blog.freelancersunion.org/2014/11/04/5-great-apps-banish-your-fear-blank-page/). Ghost's Hemingway mode only disables backspace; users noted you can still edit by other means, and developers said blocking every route would be too costly. — [Ghost changelog](https://ghost.org/blog/hemingway-mode/); [Ghost forum](https://forum.ghost.org/t/a-stricter-hemingway-mode/1188)
- [P] Dedicated drafting devices (Freewrite) deliberately omit spell check, grammar check and easy revision; reviewers call the lack of features the main selling point, while one reviewer called the device bought, used once and forgotten. — [Reviewed](https://reviewed.com/tech/content/astrohause-freewrite-alpha-review); [Entreresource](https://entreresource.com/freewrite-traveler-review/)

### Inferences
- The levers with mechanism support are: (1) remove the visible object of evaluation (prior text, flagged errors); (2) remove the *possibility* of the rigid rule ("get it right first") by making editing unavailable during the session; (3) make the session routine and low-stakes (Boice).
- Spell check during drafting: the only study found (revision task) shows no harm, but it does not test drafting flow. Given the monitoring-disruption evidence, turn it off during sessions and run it at review time.
- Delayed review: no direct evidence on the best delay. Elbow's "afterwards you can throw away lousy beginnings" and Ilys's reveal-at-goal suggest review should be a separate, deliberate mode, not a peek mid-session.
- Do not frame anything as treatment for anxiety (medical claims are out of scope); "calmer drafting" is a design claim, not a therapeutic one.

### Gaps
- No controlled study found of no-backspace drafting, or of hidden-text typing, measuring anxiety (e.g. Daly–Miller writing apprehension) or perfectionism.
- Perfectionism-specific writing studies were not found in this pass (searches returned general procrastination work).

## 8. Mindfulness and calm framing: what makes a tool feel calm (visual quiet, breathing rhythm, few choices)?

### Takeaway
Calm-technology principles (Weiser & Brown; Amber Case) translate into: use the periphery, demand minimal attention, keep the feature set minimal, and fail gracefully. Breathing-rate rhythm is a design tradition (Apple's "breathing" sleep LED) with physiological evidence for *paced slow breathing*, but no evidence that a breathing-rate visual during typing entrains breathing or calms writers. Motion should avoid onsets and flashes.

### Cited Findings
- [T] Weiser & Brown (1995): "We use 'periphery' to name what we are attuned to without attending to explicitly"; calm technology "engages both the center and the periphery of our attention"; "the periphery is informing without overburdening"; recentering something from the periphery lets us take control of it; screen-based status displays "require interpretation and attention" and do not "peripheralize well"; a good tool "will offer, but not demand". — [Weiser & Brown, "Designing Calm Technology"](https://calmtech.com/papers/designing-calm-technology)
- [T] Amber Case's calm-technology principles include: require the smallest possible amount of attention ("Technology can communicate, but doesn't need to speak"); think about failure, does it "default to a usable state"; use the minimum technology needed to solve the problem; respect social norms and introduce features slowly. — [Case, Principles of Calm Technology](https://www.caseorganic.com/post/principles-of-calm-technology)
- [P] Apple's "Breathing status LED indicator" patent: PWM-driven sinusoidal brightness instead of a blink; coverage quotes the patent saying the rhythm "mimics the rhythm of breathing which is psychologically appealing"; commentators put it near adult breathing rates of 12–20 per minute. — [US 6,658,577](https://patents.google.com/patent/US6658577); [512 Pixels 2010](https://512pixels.net/2010/08/on-sleep-indicators/)
- [E] Slow breathing (<10 breaths/min): systematic review (15 studies) finds increased heart-rate variability and respiratory sinus arrhythmia, EEG alpha increase, and psychological effects. — [Zaccaro et al. 2018, Frontiers in Human Neuroscience](https://doi.org/10.3389/fnhum.2018.00353). Meta-analysis of 31 studies (n = 1,133): immediate SBP reduction (SMD −0.45), RMSSD increase (SMD 0.37); effect on perceived stress marginal (SMD −0.51, p = .06). — [Shao, Man & Lee 2024, Mindfulness](https://doi.org/10.1007/s12671-023-02294-2)
- [E] Motion onsets capture attention; ongoing motion does not. — [Abrams & Christ 2003](https://doi.org/10.1111/1467-9280.01458)
- [P] iA Writer's Focus Mode dims everything outside the current sentence or paragraph; "Typewriter" keeps the caret vertically centred; iA advises turning Focus Mode off for editing because centring conflicts with selection. — [iA Writer support: Focus Mode](https://ia.net/writer/support/editor/focus-mode); [iPad page](https://ia.net/writer/support/editor/focus-mode/focus-mode-ipad)
- [P] OmmWriter markets calm through full screen, natural backgrounds, ambient audio and keystroke sounds; users report a calm mood (small sample of reviews). — [Capterra](https://www.capterra.co.uk/reviews/151933/ommwriter)

### Inferences
- Calm for a forward-only tool means: one surface, no chrome during a session, nothing that blinks or starts moving unprompted. The only motion is the self-caused slide of text, which is predictable and continuous. Session status (time left) belongs in the periphery: a thin, slowly growing line, not a ticking number.
- "Graceful failure" (Case) is critical here: hiding text must never mean losing it. Autosave every keystroke to disk; a crash or a closed lid must leave the draft intact. This is the calm opposite of the punishment apps.
- Few choices: a session needs at most two settings before starting (length or target, sound on/off); everything else stays in preferences.
- A breathing-rate pulse (e.g. caret or progress line at ~6 per minute) is aesthetically defensible (Apple LED tradition) but has no evidence of effect during typing and may compete with the caret. If used, use it only in idle or pause states, not while typing.

### Gaps
- No evidence found on meditation-app UI features (visual quiet, limited choice) and their effect on a concurrent productive task; not researched in depth here.
- No study found showing that a breathing-paced visual entrains breathing while typing.

## 9. Design implications for a forward-only writing tool (eyes fixed on one spot, text sliding away)

### Takeaway
The evidence supports a tool that hides editable history, keeps the eyes on a fixed frontier, gives instant informational feedback per keystroke (glyph, slide, optional quiet click under ~30 ms), keeps the session routine and low-pressure, never destroys text, and separates drafting from review. Several choices (backspace scope, how much of the frontier stays visible, horizontal vs vertical) have no direct comparative evidence; they are presented as options with trade-offs.

### Cited Findings
- [E] Hidden text cuts corrections sharply with little cost to speed or accuracy — [Pinet & Nozari 2020](https://www.cmu.edu/dietrich/psychology/nozarilab/papers/2020PinetNozari.pdf); [Kim & Tan 2014](https://microsoft.com/en-us/research/wp-content/uploads/2014/08/C63_JRKimTan_HS2014.pdf)
- [E] Text quality is little affected by suppressing visual feedback — [Olive & Piolat 2002](https://doi.org/10.1080/00207590244000089); rough drafting neither helps nor hurts quality, an outline (even mental) helps — [Kellogg 1988](https://scholarsmine.mst.edu/psysci_facwork/284)
- [E] Look-backs cluster at sentence starts and serve planning — [Torrance et al. 2016](https://irep.ntu.ac.uk/id/eprint/16307/); monitor-gazing typists are more productive — [Johansson et al. 2010](https://www.lunduniversity.lu.se/lup/publication/76c41904-2e75-40b5-bae4-39e2791a26eb)
- [E] Moving text is hard to read; 1–2-character jumps are worst — [Granaas et al. 1984](https://doi.org/10.1177/001872088402600109); motion onsets capture attention — [Abrams & Christ 2003](https://doi.org/10.1111/1467-9280.01458)
- [E] Audio feedback feels simultaneous at ~19 ms, perceived quality drops past 70–100 ms — [Kaaresoja et al. 2014](https://doi.org/10.1145/2611387)
- [E] Punishment raises output but halves ideas per page; "least pressure sufficient" — [Krashen 2002 on Boice](https://mail.sdkrashen.com/content/articles/optimal_levels.pdf)
- [E] Informational positive feedback enhances motivation; expected contingent rewards undermine it — [Deci et al. 1999](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.125.6.627); lagging progress feedback demotivates, intermittent feedback softens it — [Conrad et al. 2010](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.intcom.2010.03.001)
- [P] Existing patterns: Ilys (last keystroke only, unlock at goal) — [The Next Web](https://thenextweb.com/news/write-drunk-edit-sober); MDWA hardcore blur — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Most_Dangerous_Writing_App); Draft Hemingway Mode (append-only) — [Kontny](https://ninjasandrobots.com/draft-announcements-hemingway-mode-wordgoogle-docs-support-kindle-exports-and-more); iA Writer Focus and Typewriter modes — [iA](https://ia.net/writer/support/editor/focus-mode)

### Inferences
All of the following are the researcher's design reasoning from the findings above.

**Backspace: options and trade-offs**
- **B0, none (append-only, Draft Hemingway):** strongest separation of drafting from editing; but the inner loop still notices about half of typos (Pinet & Nozari), so a typo you feel but cannot fix may itself become a small, repeated irritant.
- **B1, within the current word only** (until space): lets the fast inner-loop correction happen (cheap, automatic), stops reaching back into finished words. Most consistent with the evidence (Quinlan: most fixes are deferred anyway; immediate in-word fixes are the motor-level kind). *Recommended default.*
- **B2, unlimited but blind** (backspace deletes characters that are no longer visible): honours habit, but deleting unseen text invites anxious rereading in one's head.
- **B3, strike-out instead of delete** (backspace marks the word for later review, does not remove it): keeps forward motion and saves the intent for the review pass; costs some implementation complexity.

**How much text stays visible**
- **V1, only the last word or keystroke (Ilys):** maximum suppression of review; costs local planning support (Torrance) and may feel disorienting for argumentative text.
- **V2, the last few words to the current clause, fading toward the left edge:** keeps local syntax in view (verbal working memory holds about a clause; Kellogg 2013 on translation needing verbal WM), removes global history. *Recommended default*, with V1 as a "deep" option.
- **V3, current sentence plus a dimmed previous one (iA Focus):** most support for planning, most invitation to reread.

**Horizontal single line vs vertical typewriter**
- **H, horizontal ticker (the user's VS Code setup):** text leaves the fovea within a few words and is hard to reread while moving (Granaas); one line means no line breaks and no reflow; the eyes never move. Matches the user's report. Make the slide smooth and continuous per keystroke (avoid 1–2-character jumps, which Granaas found worst for reading; here that only matters for how comfortable the frontier is to read), with the glyph shown in under ~30 ms and any easing short, so motion never lags input.
- **T, vertical typewriter with dimmed history (iA Typewriter + Focus):** familiar; history stays legible, so the pull to review stays. Better for writers who need to see the shape of a paragraph.
- No comparative study exists; the user's own experience favours H. Offer H by default, T as an option.

**Rest of the screen:** full screen, nothing else moving (Weiser & Brown; Case); session status in the periphery as a slowly filling line; no live word count or WPM during the session (evaluative; Conrad on lagging progress); no squiggles or autocorrect while drafting (monitoring disruption).

**Sound:** optional quiet keystroke click (default on is defensible given the user's report and users' preference for feedback over none; easy off), latency under ~30 ms, slight variation between key sounds, no louder events except a soft end-of-session cue. Ambient background audio optional, default off. No haptics (not available while typing on macOS).

**Pressure:** no deletion-on-pause. If a "keep going" cue is wanted, a gentle peripheral fade that reverses on the next keystroke (Write or Die's "gentle" mode, not "kamikaze"). Never destroy text; autosave every keystroke.

**Session length and start:** writer-chosen time box or word target, default 10–25 minutes (Elbow; Pomodoro evidence mixed); optional one-line intent before starting (the only pre-writing step with quality evidence, Kellogg 1988), shown nowhere during the session.

**Ending ritual:** a clear end (soft tone; text stops sliding); an informational summary (minutes, words) rather than rewards (Deci); the draft is saved to a log (Boice/Krashen: self-recorded output was the most effective level of management). Review happens in a separate mode the writer opens deliberately, ideally not straight away (Ilys-style reveal at goal; Elbow's "afterwards"). The best delay is unknown.

**Evaluation plan (to check these choices on the user):** log keystrokes per session (words per minute, pause lengths over 2 s, backspace rate) for H/V1/V2/T and sound on/off; after each session, rate perceived flow on a short scale and anxiety on one item; compare across a few weeks. n = 1, so treat results as personal tuning, not general evidence.

### Gaps
- No study compares horizontal single-line drafting with vertical typewriter drafting.
- No study tests how many trailing words should stay visible.
- No evidence on the best delay between drafting and review, or on whether an end-of-session ritual affects return rate.
- No evidence that the sliding-text animation itself (as opposed to hidden history) adds momentum or enjoyment; the user's report is the only data.
